Framing: ChainLens is NOT a competing detection or protection product. Hypernative and Forta are studied as reference points for what the industry already solves well. Our thesis: the under-served layer is investigation — turning a warning into a verifiable explanation. All competitor claims are sourced from their current official documentation (appendix).

The thesis — three layers, one gap

DETECTION

“Something suspicious was detected.”

Monitoring, anomaly & threat detection, risk intelligence — well served today (Forta, Hypernative, others).

PROTECTION

“Stop this transaction.”

Simulation, policy enforcement, blocking, automated response — also well served today.

INVESTIGATION

“Show me exactly why this matters.”

Evidence reconstruction · context · current exposure · observed vs inferred · human-readable explanation.

Web3 security already has powerful detection and protection. The opportunity we are exploring is the layer that turns a warning into an investigation: WHAT happened · WHY does it matter · WHAT can still be affected · WHAT evidence proves it · WHAT is observation vs inference · WHAT remains unknown.

ChainLens: from alert to evidence.

Do NOT claim detection or protection are missing from the market — both are strong. Existing tools optimize for speed and precision (Hypernative: sub-second simulation, vendor-reported <0.001% FP; Forta: community detection bots on every block). By design they compress evidence into compact verdicts. The compression is the trade-off — and the opportunity: decompress the verdict into verifiable facts. An investigation layer that consumes alerts from any source and returns verifiable reasoning.

The key question: “Why did the system warn me — and can I verify the reason myself?” Detection output is a compressed verdict; the user still needs the facts, the confidence class, and the current exposure.

Detection stays where it is — Forta bots, Hypernative engines, wallet built-ins. ChainLens consumes their output (or raw activity) and reconstructs the evidence behind the warning. What users receive today: a verdict (HIGH RISK / severity / score), a category, a recommendation. What they still need: the concrete facts behind the verdict, which facts are measured vs modeled, what is exposed right now, and a way to verify independently. These are trade-offs inherent to fast, accurate detection at scale — not failures of existing products. Investigation is a different product emphasis.

Core: evidence trail with confidence classes

Every finding → detection reason → blockchain observations → tx/block → contract state → related addresses → derived inference → current exposure.

“Potentially dangerous token approval”

OBSERVED Wallet granted spender X unlimited USDC allowance.
OBSERVED Allowance is still active.
OBSERVED Wallet currently holds $2,840 USDC.
OBSERVED Spender X is an upgradeable contract.
OBSERVED Address Y controls an upgrade/admin role.
INFERRED The approved contract may potentially affect
          the wallet's current USDC balance.
UNKNOWN  Blockchain evidence does not establish whether
          address Y intends to misuse that capability.

Per-claim provenance: data coverage · source · timestamp · chain · block. No arbitrary risk score.

Why the classes matter

  • OBSERVED directly verifiable on-chain fact — anyone can re-check it at the cited block/tx
  • INFERRED derived from observed facts — stated as possibility, never verdict
  • UNKNOWN cannot currently be established — including intent; never silently filled

Scores compress and hide; classes expose. This targets explainability gaps and the difficulty of independently verifying why an alert fired — without claiming any competitor lacks this.

Five observations, one inference, one unknown — deliberately concrete. Each observed line carries provenance metadata (chain, block, tx hash, source, timestamp). Intent can never be observed on-chain; the product says so explicitly rather than implying guilt by association. A "risk score 87" cannot be argued with; "this is observed, that is inferred, this is unknown" can be.

History vs exposure — and what we actually checked

HISTORY

“What has this address done?”
Past txs, associations, reputation, prior incidents — point-in-time intelligence.

EXPOSURE

“What can it affect RIGHT NOW?”
Active allowances, live permissions, current admin/upgrade roles — present-day blast radius.

Suspicious history ⛔ ≠ current danger verdict. Clean history ⛔ can still coexist with dangerous active approvals. Two separate axes — every ChainLens statement is labeled which one it is.

History vs exposure is a major product concept, not a UI detail. Screening products reasonably emphasize historical reputation (Hypernative Screening & Intelligence continuously re-evaluates previously approved addresses as new intelligence emerges). ChainLens adds the complementary axis: live exposure computed from current contract state. Neither axis alone answers "what can hurt me now?" On coverage: every scanner has limits — chains, historical data, known-pattern rules. Declaring coverage turns "all clear" into a scoped statement. This is true for every detection product including ours; the difference is making the boundary visible.

Clicking “Inspect Evidence” reveals the actual blockchain facts — hashes, blocks, state — not a vendor's summary. Fewer, decomposable alerts instead of more alerts.

The alert names the exposure (USD at stake), the reason chain (numbered, each independently checkable), and an entry point into raw evidence. A non-expert stops at the summary; an expert drills to the transaction hash. Same content, two altitudes — our answer to information overload. On the graph: graph analysis exists in the industry (Hypernative advertises graph analysis among its engines; Forta Risk maps dependencies several hops away). We don't claim novelty of graphs — the emphasis differs: ChainLens's graph is an investigation surface where each edge is clickable to its evidence, oriented around the user's wallet and what it enables right now.

AI is not the primary detector; the LLM never invents blockchain evidence. Good output: “The wallet currently has an unlimited USDC allowance granted to contract X. The allowance was created at transaction Y and remains active.”

Hypernative's Wallet Protection already returns "human-readable balance changes, approval grants, and risk warnings" to wallets (their docs) — the raw ingredients exist in the market. ChainLens's proposed emphasis: the dialog is evidence-linked end-to-end and ends in an informed user decision rather than a vendor verdict. Don't claim wallets lack pre-signing explanation — claim deeper investigation behind the explanation. On AI: deterministic layer (allowance reads, proxy/admin extraction, flow tracing) is reproducible; the LLM only renders the evidence bundle in natural language, each sentence grounded in an evidence id. If it can't ground a sentence, the sentence isn't shown. AI is the narrator, not the witness.

Competitive landscape — primary product emphasis

Forta — documented: detection bots on every block → IPFS alert registry (App/API); community bot ecosystem; Firewall = pre-inclusion screening for integrated rollups (RaaS) & protocols (attestations); Risk = dependency mapping “several hops away, quantified in USD.” Inferred trade-offs: alert content is bot-developer-defined; bot logic may be private/obfuscated; infrastructure-side emphasis — event detection, not end-user investigation. (docs.forta.network; forta.org)

Hypernative — documented: monitoring + automated response, 75+ chains / 300+ risk types; Transaction Guard pre-signing simulation + policy engine; Wallet Protection API (human-readable balance changes, approval grants, warnings); Screening & Intelligence with multi-hop tracing, audit trails. Inferred trade-offs: enterprise/API shape → end-user evidence depth is integrator-mediated; verdict-shaped output (scores, APPROVE/DENY); proprietary detection logic; reputation/history-driven screening. (hypernative.io product pages)
